What “maximum variance along PC1” means

Take a 2-feature cloud (the same idea holds in 100 dimensions). A “direction” is a line through the middle of the data. Every sample can be projected onto that line — it collapses to a single number, its score. PCA asks: which direction makes those projected scores spread out the most? Drag the slider and watch.

So “maximum variance” = “best summary line”

One direction is better than another when the projected samples spread out more on it — because then the scores genuinely distinguish the samples from each other. The same direction is also the line that passes closest to all the points (least perpendicular error). Maximizing variance kept and minimizing information thrown away are two views of the same optimum, which is why keeping the top PCs is the least-distorting compression of the data.

02

Orthogonality is built in, not luck

Once PC1 is found, PC2 is defined as the variance-maximizing direction subject to being uncorrelated with PC1 — and “uncorrelated scores” means the weight vectors are perpendicular. The right angle you see below is guaranteed by the eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix.

The two principal components of the cloud
PC1 (rose) is the long axis of the 1σ ellipse; PC2 (teal) is the short one — exactly 90° apart. The data cloud is an ellipsoid in high dimensions; the PCs are its axes.

Why the eigenvectors must be perpendicular

1 · Symmetry. The covariance matrix C is symmetric (cov of feature A with B = cov of B with A). The spectral theorem says every real symmetric matrix has a full set of mutually orthogonal eigenvectors. PCA just takes those eigenvectors, ordered by eigenvalue.

2 · Constraint. PC2 maximizes variance subject to w₂·w₁ = 0. Zero dot product is the definition of perpendicular. It’s not that orthogonality emerges — it’s imposed at every step, then the Lagrange-multiplier math hands you back an eigenvector.

3 · SVD. Libraries actually compute X = UΣVᵞ; the columns of V are the PCs, and SVD produces orthonormal columns by construction.

One edge case: equal eigenvalues

If two eigenvalues are exactly equal (e.g. isotropic, noise-like data), the PCs inside that subspace are not unique — any rotation of them explains the same variance. You can still always choose them orthogonal, but which particular pair a library returns is arbitrary. Well-separated eigenvalues → unique, stable PCs.

So what does a dot in a PC1 vs PC2 plot mean?

It is one sample (row) of your data — the same patient / image / customer as before — summarized by two numbers instead of 100: where it sits along the dataset’s biggest pattern (PC1) and its second-biggest, independent pattern (PC2). Dots that cluster together are samples whose 100 features behaved similarly; a far-out dot is an unusual sample.

Distances are approximate

The 2D plot faithfully preserves only what PC1+PC2 capture (32% here). Two samples far apart on screen are genuinely different; but two samples that look close might differ on PC3+.

Standardize first

Variance is unit-dependent. Without standardization, one feature measured on a large scale can dominate PC1 all by itself — and you’ve merely rediscovered that feature, not a pattern.

Axes are unsupervised

Nothing guarantees PC1/PC2 align with your class labels or outcome. Sometimes the structure you care about lives on PC5 — or in none of them. Check the scree plot before trusting the picture.

Quick reference
  • Each dot = one sample (row) of the original data — nothing is averaged or dropped.
  • Dot position = that sample’s scores on the two highest-variance patterns of all 100 features.
  • Clusters = groups of samples that behave similarly across the features; outliers = atypical samples.
  • PCs = orthogonal linear combinations of the features; PC1 maximizes projected variance, each next PC maximizes what’s left under the orthogonality constraint.
  • Loadings = the per-feature weights that tell you which features drive each axis.
